Worth the Wait

Quarterbacks keep getting better long after their teams stop waiting on them.

By Your NameData: nflverse play-by-play, 2000–2025

This report follows every NFL quarterback who entered the league from 2000 on and went on to start at least half a season: 135 quarterbacks and 105,862 of their drives, through the 2025 season. Quarterbacks are still improving in their fifth and sixth seasons. Their turnovers do not come down until Year 6, and most of the quarterbacks whose best season came in Year 5 or later had looked like write-offs early. Yet most teams give up on the quarterback they started with by Year 4, and nearly half of the quarterbacks who went on to start somewhere else had their best season after leaving. The learning curve is longer than teams are willing to wait.

Part 1 · The first quarter

The learning curve is long

If quarterbacks were finished products after a year or two, a short leash would make sense. They are not. On turnovers, on efficiency, and on when their best seasons arrive, many quarterbacks are still improving in Years 5 and 6.

Finding 01

Turnovers don't really fall until Year 6

A rookie quarterback gives the ball away, through an interception or a fumble he loses, about 10.9 times every 100 drives. For the next four seasons that barely moves: 10.4, 10.6, 10.7 and 10.7. None of those years is statistically different from the rookie year (every p-value is above 0.2).

Year 6 is the first real drop, to 9.5 per 100 drives (p = 0.002 against the rookie year). That is about 13% fewer giveaways, and it takes five full seasons to get there. It stays below the rookie level after that, though Years 8 and 9 drift back toward 10.

Finding 02

Most late breakouts came from quarterbacks who looked like write-offs

Call a quarterback's best season his breakout: his highest EPA per play compared with the league average, in a season with 8+ starts. Among the 73 quarterbacks whose careers lasted at least eight seasons, plenty broke out early: 30 had their best season before Year 5, including Patrick Mahomes, Lamar Jackson and Cam Newton in Year 2. Early stars are real.

The quarterbacks this report is about are the other 43, whose breakout came in Year 5 or later. Longer careers simply have more seasons to peak in, so the point is not how many there are. It is who they were: 30 of the 43 looked like write-offs early. 15 were below the league average over Years 1–3, and 15 were not even starting yet. Drew Brees, Aaron Rodgers, Matthew Stafford, Ryan Tannehill, Alex Smith, Sam Darnold and Geno Smith are all on that list. A team grading them after three seasons would have seen a quarterback to replace.

Finding 03

Most good quarterbacks show it early, but a third are late bloomers

Of the 104 quarterbacks with at least one starting season, 63 eventually played a season better than the league average. Most of them, 41, did it by Year 3. Teams are right that good quarterbacks often show it early.

But 22 of the 63 (35%) did not get there until Year 4 or later, and 15 not until Year 5 or later. That group includes Aaron Rodgers, Drew Brees, Tony Romo and Kirk Cousins in Year 4, Alex Smith and Sam Darnold in Year 7, and Geno Smith in Year 10. A team that judges its quarterback after three seasons never sees any of them become above average.

Finding 04

Rookie to Year 2 is the biggest jump, but it isn't the peak

This finding uses EPA, expected points added. Before every play, based on the down, the distance and the spot on the field, an offense can expect to score a certain number of points on that drive. EPA is how much a play changes that expectation: a completion that converts a third down adds expected points, and a sack or an interception takes them away. Here it is averaged per play and compared with the league average that season, so 0 means a league-average quarterback.

Following the same 58 quarterbacks (everyone who started 8+ games in Year 5), the jump from rookie year to Year 2 is by far the largest: from 0.069 below average per play to 0.035 above. 74% of quarterbacks improve from Year 1 to Year 2, more than in any other year. But the group keeps climbing after that, dips in Years 4 and 5, and is at its best in Year 8 (+0.061). Judging a quarterback by his second season catches him at the start of the climb.

Part 2 · The second quarter

Teams don't wait that long

Now the other side of the argument: how long does a team stick with the quarterback it started with? Here, a quarterback's "original team" is the first team he played for.

Finding 05

Most teams give up on their quarterback by Year 4

For 88 quarterbacks who started for their original team and are no longer its starter, I found the last season they started there. The median is Year 4. 61% were done by Year 4 and 73% by Year 5. The single most common last season is Year 3.

In other words, most original teams moved on before the quarterback's turnovers came down (Finding 01) and before the late bloomers in Findings 02 and 03 arrived.

Finding 06

Only about a third are still starting for their original team in Year 5

Take the 73 quarterbacks who were starters in their first or second season. In Year 2, 81% of them are starting for their original team. By Year 4 it is 44%, by Year 5 36%, and by Year 6 just 22%.

The gap between the two lines is quarterbacks who are still starting, just not for the team that brought them in. By Year 6, 38% are starting somewhere, but only 22% for their first team.

Finding 07

The most common number of starting seasons a team gives its quarterback is one

Of those same 88 quarterbacks, 30 (34%) got exactly one starting season from their original team before it moved on. Another 15 got two and 7 got three: 59% got three seasons or fewer.

A handful got more than a decade, but they are the exceptions. The typical quarterback gets a short run to prove himself, shorter than Part 1 says he needs.

Part 3 · The third quarter

The cost of not waiting

If the curve is long and teams are short on patience, some quarterbacks should blossom after their first team gives up on them, and the ones who were given time should improve. Both show up. Click any quarterback below to open his career in the explorer.

Finding 08

21 quarterbacks had their best season after their first team gave up on them

Of the 44 quarterbacks who started for two or more teams, 21 had their best season for a later team, and 18 of those seasons were better than the league average.

Drew Brees left the Chargers after Year 5 and had his best season in New Orleans in Year 9 (13–2). Jared Goff's best came in Detroit in Year 9 (15–2). Ryan Tannehill went from barely above average in Miami to +0.20 per play in Tennessee. Sam Darnold and Geno Smith were below average with the Jets, and both later had above-average seasons in Seattle, including Darnold's 14–3 season in 2025.

Finding 09

Quarterbacks who were kept through rough early years became above average

Nine quarterbacks were below the league average in Years 1–3 and were still starting for their original team in Year 5. As a group they went from 0.066 below average to 0.065 above it from Year 4 on. Their win rate rose from .427 to .552, and their interception rate fell from 2.8% to 2.0%.

All nine improved, and six became above average, including Drew Brees, Matthew Stafford, Alex Smith and Derek Carr. It is a small group, but it is exactly the group a short leash would have cut.

Part 4 · The fourth quarter

The case against waiting

The strongest objection to this argument is that early struggles usually mean what they seem to mean. The data partly agrees.

Finding 10

Most early strugglers never become above average, but most never got another chance

Here is the strongest case against waiting. Of the 53 quarterbacks who were below the league average over their starting seasons in Years 1–3, only 18 (34%) ever had an above-average season after that. Of the 32 who were above average early, 25 (78%) had one later. Early play does predict later play, and most teams that move on from a struggling quarterback are playing the odds.

But look at why the other 35 never got there. 28 of them never started another season after Year 3, and only 7 got more chances and still never beat the average. Among early strugglers who were given more starts, 18 of 25 (72%) eventually did. The data cannot say how the other 28 would have turned out. They were never given the time.

About the data

Everything on this site is computed from free, public data by the scripts in the GitHub repository.

Where it comes from

nflverse play-by-play for the 2000–2025 NFL seasons, plus its schedule (final scores and listed starting QBs), player (rookie season, draft, full names), roster (jersey numbers) and team files. The data is released under CC-BY 4.0.

What one row is

The data set, data/qb_drives.csv, has one row per quarterback per offensive drive: 105,862 rows and 41 columns. Each row has the game, date, season, the quarterback and his career year, the teams, the quarter, how the drive ended, where it started, the game's final result, and the quarterback's own passing, rushing, sack, turnover and EPA totals on that drive.

Which quarterbacks, and what was dropped

  • Quarterbacks whose first NFL season was 2000 or later and who started 9+ regular-season games in at least one season. Quarterbacks who entered the league before 2000 are left out so every career is followed from its first season. All of each quarterback's seasons through 2025 are kept, including seasons as a backup.
  • The 2026 season is left out because it is still in progress.
  • Kneels and spikes are dropped (they run the clock and are not real attempts), and so are two-point tries. A drive made only of kneels has no row.
  • If a quarterback was replaced mid-drive, each quarterback gets his own row for that drive.
  • All findings use the regular season only.
  • Air yards are blank before 2006, when the NFL did not track them.

How each number is computed

Career year
Season minus the quarterback's rookie season, plus one. Year 1 is the rookie season.
Starting season
A season with 8 or more starts, where a start is being the team's listed starting quarterback.
Turnovers per 100 drives
(Interceptions + fumbles the quarterback lost) ÷ drives × 100. The confidence band is ±1.96 × √turnovers ÷ drives × 100.
EPA per play
Expected points added (nflverse's measure of how much a play changes the offense's expected points on the drive), summed over the quarterback's own plays and divided by his number of plays.
EPA vs league
A season's EPA per play minus that season's average EPA per play for every NFL quarterback, so seasons from different eras compare fairly. 0 is league average; above 0 is an above-average season.
Breakout season
A quarterback's starting season with the highest EPA vs league.
Early (Years 1–3)
EPA vs league pooled over all of a quarterback's starting seasons in Years 1–3, for any team. "Looked like a write-off" means that number is below 0, or he had no starting season in Years 1–3.
Original team
The first team the quarterback played for. This handles draft-day trades.
Kept
Still starting for his original team in Year 5 or later (Finding 09).
Win %
(Wins + half of ties) ÷ starts, counting only games the quarterback started.
Group averages
Pooled over the group's plays, drives or starts (for example, total turnovers ÷ total drives), not an average of averages.
Significance
A two-proportion z-test of each year's turnover rate against the rookie year. It treats drives as independent, so it slightly overstates confidence.
Explorer percentiles
The share of the 625 starting seasons in the data that a season beats on that stat (for interception rate, lower is better).
Jersey colors
Approximate home-jersey colors for each team and era, entered by hand from uniform history.

Limits

Later career years only include quarterbacks who lasted that long, so a plain average by career year flatters later years. That is why Findings 02–04 compare each quarterback with himself or follow a fixed group. Classes from 2019 on have not had time to reach Year 8, so they are left out of the career-length findings, and rookie classes 2022–2025 are still early in their careers.
