Worth the Wait

By Your Name · Data: nflverse play-by-play

This report follows every NFL quarterback who entered the league from 2000 on and went on to start at least half a season: 135 quarterbacks and 105,862 of their drives, through the 2025 season. The pattern is consistent. Quarterbacks keep getting better well into their fifth and sixth seasons. Their turnovers do not drop until Year 6, and the typical quarterback's best season comes in Year 5. But most teams give up on the quarterback they started with by Year 4, and nearly half of the quarterbacks who later started somewhere else had their best season after leaving. The learning curve is longer than teams are willing to wait.

1st Quarter The learning curve is long

If quarterbacks were finished products after a year or two, a short leash would make sense. They are not. On turnovers, on efficiency, and on the timing of their best seasons, the typical quarterback is still improving in Years 5 and 6.

Finding 01

Turnovers don't really fall until Year 6

Rookies throw an interception on 2.63% of their passes. In Years 2 through 5 that barely moves: 2.48%, 2.45%, 2.45%, 2.44%. None of those four seasons is statistically different from the rookie year (every p-value is above 0.1).

Then in Year 6 the rate drops to 2.21%, clearly below the rookie rate (p < 0.001), and it stays lower from there. Counting lost fumbles too, turnovers fall from 10.7 per 100 drives in Year 5 to 9.5 in Year 6. That is about 16% fewer interceptions than a rookie throws, and it takes five full seasons to get there.

Finding 02

The typical quarterback has his best season in Year 5

For each quarterback whose career lasted at least eight seasons, I found his single best starting season, measured by EPA per play compared with that year's league average. The median best season is Year 5.

59% of these quarterbacks (43 of 73) had their best season in Year 5 or later, and 48% in Year 6 or later. Year 2 is the most common single year (13 QBs), but most careers peak later than that.

Finding 03

A third of the QBs who become above average don't get there until Year 4 or later

Of the 104 quarterbacks who had at least one starting season, 63 eventually played a season better than the league average. For most of them it happened early, but 35% (22 QBs) first got there in Year 4 or later, and 24% in Year 5 or later.

A team that judges its quarterback after three seasons never sees any of those 22 good seasons. The other 41 quarterbacks never had an above-average season at all.

Finding 04

Rookie to Year 2 is the biggest jump, but it isn't the peak

Following the same 58 quarterbacks (everyone who started 8+ games in Year 5), the jump from rookie year to Year 2 is by far the largest: from 0.069 below the league average per play to 0.035 above it. 74% of quarterbacks improve from Year 1 to Year 2, more than in any other year.

But Year 2 is a starting point, not an arrival. The group keeps climbing and is best in Year 8 (+0.061), with a dip in Years 4 and 5 along the way. Judging a quarterback by his second season catches him at the very start of the climb.

2nd Quarter Teams don't wait that long

Now the other side of the argument: how long does a team actually stick with the quarterback it started with? Here, a quarterback's "original team" is the first team he played for.

Finding 05

Most teams give up on their quarterback by Year 4

For 88 quarterbacks who started for their original team and are no longer its starter, I found the last season they started there. The median is Year 4. 61% were done by Year 4 and 73% by Year 5. The single most common last season is Year 3.

In other words, most original teams moved on before the season that is most likely to be the quarterback's best (Finding 02), and before his turnovers come down (Finding 01).

Finding 06

Only about a third are still starting for their original team in Year 5

Take the 73 quarterbacks who were starters in their first or second season. In Year 2, 81% of them are starting for their original team. By Year 4 it is 44%, by Year 5 36%, and by Year 6 just 22%.

The gap between the two lines is quarterbacks who are still starting, just not for the team that brought them in. By Year 6, 38% are starting somewhere, but only 22% for their first team.

Finding 07

The most common number of starting seasons a team gives its quarterback is one

Of those same 88 quarterbacks, 30 (34%) got exactly one starting season from their original team before it moved on. Another 15 got two and 7 got three: 59% got three seasons or fewer.

A handful got more than a decade, but they are the exceptions. The typical quarterback gets a short run to prove himself, shorter than the curve in the 1st Quarter says he needs.

3rd Quarter The cost of not waiting

If the curve is long and teams are short on patience, some quarterbacks should blossom after their first team gives up on them, and the ones who were given time should improve. Both show up. Click any quarterback below to open his career in the explorer.

Finding 08

21 quarterbacks had their best season after their first team gave up on them

Of the 44 quarterbacks who started for two or more teams, 21 had their best season for a later team, and 18 of those seasons were better than the league average.

Drew Brees left the Chargers after Year 5 and had his best season in New Orleans in Year 9 (13–2). Jared Goff's best came in Detroit in Year 9 (15–2). Ryan Tannehill went from barely above average in Miami to +0.20 per play in Tennessee. Sam Darnold and Geno Smith were below average with the Jets, and both later had above-average seasons in Seattle, including Darnold's 14–3 season in 2025.

Finding 09

Quarterbacks who were kept through rough early years became above average

Nine quarterbacks were below the league average in Years 1–3 and were still starting for their original team in Year 5. As a group they went from 0.066 below average to 0.065 above it from Year 4 on. Their win rate rose from .427 to .552, and their interception rate fell from 2.8% to 2.0%.

All nine improved, and six became above average, including Drew Brees, Matthew Stafford, Alex Smith and Derek Carr. It is a small group, but it is exactly the group a short leash would have cut.

4th Quarter The case against

The strongest objection to this argument is that teams let go of the quarterbacks who deserved it. The data partly agrees.

Finding 10

Teams mostly let go of the right quarterbacks

The 52 quarterbacks whose original team stopped starting them before Year 5 were clearly worse in Years 1–3 than the 32 who were kept: 0.077 below average per play against 0.041 above, and a .427 win rate against .592. The 18 who later started elsewhere improved, but on average only to 0.040 below average.

So teams are not wrong about most of the quarterbacks they cut. The argument of this report is narrower: the curve is long enough that a short leash also cuts some quarterbacks who were still on their way up, as Findings 08 and 09 show. Even among the quarterbacks who were below average early, the ones who were kept had been less far below it (−0.066) than the ones who were let go (−0.118).

About the data

Everything on this site is computed from free, public data by the scripts in the GitHub repository.

Where it comes from

nflverse play-by-play for the 2000–2025 NFL seasons, plus its schedule (final scores and listed starting QBs), player (rookie season, draft, full names), roster (jersey numbers) and team files. The data is released under CC-BY 4.0.

What one row is

The data set, data/qb_drives.csv, has one row per quarterback per offensive drive: 105,862 rows and 41 columns. Each row has the game, date, season, the quarterback and his career year, the teams, the quarter, how the drive ended, where it started, the game's final result, and the quarterback's own passing, rushing, sack, turnover and EPA totals on that drive.

Which quarterbacks, and what was dropped

  • Quarterbacks whose first NFL season was 2000 or later and who started 9+ regular-season games in at least one season. Quarterbacks who entered the league before 2000 are left out so every career is followed from its first season. All of each quarterback's seasons through 2025 are kept, including seasons as a backup.
  • The 2026 season is left out because it is still in progress.
  • Kneels and spikes are dropped (they run the clock and are not real attempts), and so are two-point tries. A drive made only of kneels has no row.
  • If a quarterback was replaced mid-drive, each quarterback gets his own row for that drive.
  • All findings use the regular season only.
  • Air yards are blank before 2006, when the NFL did not track them.

How each number is computed

Career year
Season minus the quarterback's rookie season, plus one. Year 1 is the rookie season.
Starting season
A season with 8 or more starts, where a start is being the team's listed starting quarterback.
Win %
(Wins + half of ties) ÷ starts, counting only games the quarterback started.
EPA per play
Expected points added (nflverse's measure of how much a play changes the team's expected score), summed over the quarterback's own plays and divided by his number of plays.
EPA vs league
A season's EPA per play minus that season's average EPA per play for every NFL quarterback, so seasons from different eras compare fairly. 0 is league average.
Interception rate
Interceptions ÷ pass attempts.
Turnovers per 100 drives
(Interceptions + fumbles the quarterback lost) ÷ drives × 100.
TD drives
Share of his drives that ended in a touchdown.
Best season
The starting season with the highest EPA vs league.
Original team
The first team the quarterback played for. This handles draft-day trades.
Kept / let go
Kept = still starting for his original team in Year 5 or later. Let go = started for it in Years 1–3 but not after Year 4.
Group averages
Pooled over the group's plays, drives or starts (for example, total interceptions ÷ total attempts), not an average of averages.
Significance
A two-proportion z-test of each year's interception rate against the rookie year. It treats passes as independent, so it slightly overstates confidence.
Explorer percentiles
The share of the 625 starting seasons in the data that a season beats on that stat (for interception rate, lower is better).
Jersey colors
Approximate home-jersey colors for each team and era, entered by hand from uniform history.

Limits

Later career years only include quarterbacks who lasted that long, so a plain average by career year flatters later years. That is why Findings 02–04 compare each quarterback with himself or follow a fixed group. Classes from 2019 on have not had time to reach Year 8, so they are left out of the career-length findings. Rookie classes 2022–2025 are still early in their careers.
