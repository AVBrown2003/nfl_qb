Worth the Wait

Quarterbacks keep getting better long after their teams stop waiting on them.

By Your NameData: nflverse play-by-play and contracts, 2000–2025

This report follows every NFL quarterback who entered the league from 2000 on and went on to start at least half a season: 135 quarterbacks and 105,862 of their drives, through the 2025 season. Quarterbacks are still improving in their fifth and sixth seasons. Their turnovers do not come down until Year 6, and most of the quarterbacks whose best season came in Year 5 or later had been written off or were not yet starting early on. But the money forces an earlier decision: a quarterback's second contract now costs about a fifth of the salary cap and usually comes due after just three seasons. Most teams move on from the quarterback they started with by Year 4, and nearly half of the quarterbacks who went on to start somewhere else had their best season after leaving. The learning curve is longer than teams are willing to wait.

Part 1 · The first quarter

The learning curve is long

If quarterbacks were finished products after a year or two, a short leash would make sense. They are not. On turnovers, on efficiency, and on when their best seasons arrive, many quarterbacks are still improving in Years 5 and 6.

Finding 01

Turnovers don't really fall until Year 6

A rookie quarterback gives the ball away, through an interception or a fumble he loses, about 10.9 times every 100 drives. For the next four seasons that barely moves: 10.4, 10.6, 10.7 and 10.7. None of those years is statistically different from the rookie year (every p-value is above 0.2).

Year 6 is the first real drop, to 9.5 per 100 drives (p = 0.002 against the rookie year). That is about 13% fewer giveaways, and it takes five full seasons to get there. It stays below the rookie level after that, though Years 8 and 9 drift back toward 10.

Turnovers per 100 drives, by career year

All 135 QBs, regular season. The shaded band is a 95% confidence interval.

How it's calculated

Turnovers per 100 drives = (interceptions + fumbles the QB lost) ÷ drives × 100

Out of every 100 possessions a quarterback runs, how many end with him giving the ball to the other team. Each career year pools every quarterback in that year of his career. The band shows how much the number could move by chance: ± 1.96 × √turnovers ÷ drives × 100. The p-value compares each year with the rookie year (a two-proportion z-test); below 0.05 means the difference is unlikely to be chance.

Finding 02

Most late best seasons came from quarterbacks who were written off or waiting their turn

A quarterback's best season is the one where his EPA per play was furthest above the league average (both are explained below the chart). Among the 73 quarterbacks whose careers lasted at least eight seasons, 30 had their best season before Year 5, including Patrick Mahomes, Lamar Jackson and Cam Newton in Year 2. Early stars are real, but where do the rest fall?

43 had their best season in Year 5 or later. Longer careers have more seasons to peak in, so the count is not the point; who they were is. 15 were write-offs: they started in Years 1–3 and played below average, like Drew Brees, Matthew Stafford, Ryan Tannehill, Alex Smith, Sam Darnold and Geno Smith. 15 more were understudies: they were not starting yet, usually because they were behind an established veteran, like Aaron Rodgers behind Brett Favre or Kirk Cousins behind Robert Griffin III. Only 13 of the 43 were good from the start.

What EPA means

To compare seasons, this report uses EPA, expected points added, the standard way analysts grade a single play. Before every snap, the down, the distance and the spot on the field say how many points an offense can expect to score on that drive: first-and-10 at your own 25 is worth about 1.1 points, first-and-10 at the opponent's 25 about 4.1. A play's EPA is how much it changes that number. From 2021 to 2025, a touchdown pass averaged +2.8, a third-down completion that moved the chains +2.4, an incompletion −0.9, a sack −1.8 and an interception −4.4. Averaged per play, it rewards the plays that help a team score and punishes the ones that hurt, whatever the box score says.

How it's calculated

EPA per play = sum of EPA on the QB's plays ÷ number of his plays

EPA vs league = his EPA per play − league EPA per play that season

His plays are his passes, sacks, scrambles and designed runs. The league figure pools every NFL quarterback's plays that season, so a 2005 season and a 2024 season are each judged against their own year. The best season is his season with 8+ starts that has the highest EPA vs league. Write-off: his EPA vs league, pooled over all his starting seasons in Years 1–3, is below 0. Understudy: he had no season with 8+ starts in Years 1–3.

Finding 03

Most good quarterbacks show it early, but a third are late bloomers

Of the 104 quarterbacks with at least one starting season, 63 eventually had an above-average season. Most of them, 41, did it by Year 3. Teams are right that good quarterbacks often show it early. But 22 of the 63 (35%) did not get there until Year 4 or later, and 15 not until Year 5 or later: Aaron Rodgers, Drew Brees, Tony Romo and Kirk Cousins in Year 4, Alex Smith and Sam Darnold in Year 7, Geno Smith in Year 10. A team that judges its quarterback after three seasons never sees any of them become above average.

What league average means

Here, league average means the average EPA per play of every NFL quarterback in that season, starters and backups together. In 2024 it was +0.047 per play; in 2003 it was −0.027. A season is above average when a quarterback's EPA per play beats that season's number, which puts him ahead of the typical NFL passer that year.

How it's calculated

League average (season) = sum of EPA on every NFL QB's plays ÷ number of those plays

Above average = his EPA per play > league average, in a season with 8+ starts

For each quarterback, the chart places him at the first career year in which he had an above-average starting season. The 41 quarterbacks who never had one are not in the chart.

Finding 04

Rookie to Year 2 is the biggest jump, but it isn't the peak

Follow the same 58 quarterbacks (everyone who started 8+ games in Year 5) through their careers, measured by EPA per play against the league average (defined under the Finding 02 chart). The jump from rookie year to Year 2 is by far the largest: from 0.069 below average per play to 0.035 above. 74% of quarterbacks improve from Year 1 to Year 2, more than in any other year.

But the group keeps climbing after that, dips in Years 4 and 5, and is at its best in Year 8 (+0.061). Judging a quarterback by his second season catches him at the start of the climb.

Part 2 · The second quarter

Teams don't wait that long

Now the other side of the argument: how long does a team stick with the quarterback it started with, and what does the money have to do with it? Here, a quarterback's "original team" is the first team he played for.

Finding 05

Most teams give up on their quarterback by Year 4

For 88 quarterbacks who started for their original team and are no longer its starter, I found the last season they started there. The median is Year 4. 61% were done by Year 4 and 73% by Year 5. The single most common last season is Year 3.

In other words, most original teams moved on before the quarterback's turnovers came down (Finding 01) and before the late bloomers in Findings 02 and 03 arrived.

Finding 06

The most common number of starting seasons a team gives its quarterback is one

Of those same 88 quarterbacks, 30 (34%) got exactly one starting season from their original team before it moved on. Another 15 got two and 7 got three: 59% got three seasons or fewer.

A handful got more than a decade, but they are the exceptions. The typical quarterback gets a short run to prove himself, shorter than Part 1 says he needs.

Finding 07

The money forces the decision before the learning curve plays out

The clock. A first-round quarterback's rookie deal costs his team about 3.5% of the salary cap each year (a second-rounder about 1%). Keeping him after that costs far more. Of the 24 quarterbacks who entered the league since 2011 and got a big second contract (10%+ of the cap) from their own team, 16 signed it before Year 4, after only three seasons, and none after Year 6.

The price. Those deals have grown from a median 14% of the cap (2014–16) to about 22% (2020–25), a fifth of the whole roster's budget on one player. Who gets paid. 17 of 22 quarterbacks who were above average in Years 1–3 got one, but only 4 of 29 who were below average early: Ryan Tannehill, Derek Carr, Daniel Jones and Trevor Lawrence got deals. 12 teams never gave a big second contract to a quarterback they started with.

Put together: teams have to decide whether to commit a fifth of their cap after three seasons, before the Year 5–6 improvement in Part 1, and they almost never pay a quarterback who struggled early. That is a strong reason to move on quickly, and it is about money, not about how good the quarterback could become.

Where the contract data comes from

This finding only covers quarterbacks who entered the league since 2011. The 2011 labor agreement created today's rookie wage scale, with four-year rookie contracts and a fifth-year team option for first-round picks, so every quarterback since then has faced the same contract clock. Contract records from before about 2010 are also incomplete. Contracts come from OverTheCap, via nflverse, and money is measured as a share of that year's salary cap so deals from different years compare fairly.

How it's calculated

Cap share = contract's average money per year ÷ that year's salary cap

Career year signed = year signed − rookie year + 1

A big second contract is the first contract after his rookie deal, from his original team, worth 10% or more of the cap per year. Year 4 means it was signed before his fourth season, after three seasons of play. "Who gets paid" covers the 51 quarterbacks who entered the league 2011–2021 who started for their original team in Years 1–3, using the same above/below-average test as Finding 02.

Part 3 · The third quarter

The cost of not waiting

If the curve is long and teams are short on patience, some quarterbacks should blossom after their first team gives up on them, and the ones who are given time should improve. Both show up. Click any quarterback card below to open his career in the explorer.

Finding 08

21 quarterbacks had their best season after their first team gave up on them

Of the 44 quarterbacks who started for two or more teams, 21 had their best season for a later team, and 18 of those seasons were better than the league average.

Drew Brees left the Chargers after Year 5 and had his best season in New Orleans in Year 9 (13–2). Jared Goff's best came in Detroit in Year 9 (15–2). Ryan Tannehill went from barely above average in Miami to +0.20 per play in Tennessee. Sam Darnold and Geno Smith were below average with the Jets, and both later had above-average seasons in Seattle, including Darnold's 14–3 season in 2025.

Finding 09

Given time, the early strugglers' box scores caught up

25 quarterbacks were below average in Years 1–3 but kept getting starts afterward, including Drew Brees, Matthew Stafford, Alex Smith, Ryan Tannehill, Derek Carr and Sam Darnold. In Years 1–3 they completed 55.1% of their passes for 203 yards and 1.12 touchdown passes per game. From Year 4 on: 61.2%, 241 yards and 1.49 touchdowns per game.

The 29 quarterbacks who were good early improved only a little over the same span (58.5% to 60.3%, 233 to 250 yards, 1.56 to 1.64 touchdowns), so the strugglers closed most of the gap and passed them on completion percentage. Passing numbers rose across the league over these years, which lifts both groups; the point is the gap closing.

Part 4 · The fourth quarter

The case against waiting

The strongest objection to this argument is that early struggles, and a low draft spot, usually mean what they seem to mean. The data partly agrees.

Finding 10

Most early strugglers never become above average, but first-round "busts" often did

Of the 53 quarterbacks who were below average in Years 1–3, only 18 (34%) ever had an above-average season after that. Of the 32 who were above average early, 25 (78%) did. Early play does predict later play, and draft position sharpens it: among the early strugglers, 2 of 11 quarterbacks taken in Round 4 or later (or undrafted) became above average, and 9 of them never started another season. A late pick who struggles early is usually what he looks like.

First-round picks are different. 11 of the 25 first-rounders who struggled early later became above average, including Sam Darnold, Matthew Stafford, Ryan Tannehill, Alex Smith, Mitchell Trubisky and Teddy Bridgewater. They were drafted high for a reason, were called busts, and still had more to give. And across all early strugglers, 28 of 53 never started another season; among those who did get more starts, 18 of 25 (72%) eventually had an above-average season. The ones who were cut never got the chance to show it.

About the data

Everything on this site is computed from free, public data by the scripts in the GitHub repository.

Where it comes from

nflverse play-by-play for the 2000–2025 NFL seasons, plus its schedule (final scores and listed starting QBs), player (rookie season, draft, full names), roster (jersey numbers), team and contract files. The contract file is OverTheCap's contract history. The data is released under CC-BY 4.0.

What one row is

The data set, data/qb_drives.csv, has one row per quarterback per offensive drive: 105,862 rows and 41 columns. Each row has the game, date, season, the quarterback and his career year, the teams, the quarter, how the drive ended, where it started, the game's final result, and the quarterback's own passing, rushing, sack, turnover and EPA totals on that drive.

Which quarterbacks, and what was dropped

  • Quarterbacks whose first NFL season was 2000 or later and who started 9+ regular-season games in at least one season. Quarterbacks who entered the league before 2000 are left out so every career is followed from its first season. All of each quarterback's seasons through 2025 are kept, including seasons as a backup.
  • The 2026 season is left out because it is still in progress.
  • Kneels and spikes are dropped (they run the clock and are not real attempts), and so are two-point tries. A drive made only of kneels has no row.
  • If a quarterback was replaced mid-drive, each quarterback gets his own row for that drive.
  • All findings use the regular season only. The contract finding covers quarterbacks who entered the league from 2011 on.
  • Air yards are blank before 2006, when the NFL did not track them.

Terms used throughout

Career year
Season minus the quarterback's rookie season, plus one. Year 1 is the rookie season.
Starting season
A season with 8 or more starts, where a start is being the team's listed starting quarterback.
EPA
Expected points added: how much a play changes the number of points the offense can expect to score on the drive, based on down, distance and field position (nflverse's model).
League average
The average EPA per play of every NFL quarterback in a season. EPA vs league is a quarterback's EPA per play minus that number; above 0 is above average.
Best season
A quarterback's starting season with the highest EPA vs league.
Write-off / understudy
Write-off: started in Years 1–3 and was below average over those seasons. Understudy: no starting season in Years 1–3.
Original team
The first team the quarterback played for. This handles draft-day trades.
Big second contract
The first contract after the rookie deal, from the original team, worth 10%+ of the salary cap per year.
Group averages
Pooled over the group's plays, drives, games or attempts (for example, total turnovers ÷ total drives), not an average of averages.
Significance
A two-proportion z-test of each year's turnover rate against the rookie year. It treats drives as independent, so it slightly overstates confidence.
Explorer percentiles
The share of the 625 starting seasons in the data that a season beats on that stat (for interception rate, lower is better).
Jersey colors
Approximate home-jersey colors for each team and era, entered by hand from uniform history.

Limits

Later career years only include quarterbacks who lasted that long, so a plain average by career year flatters later years. That is why several findings compare each quarterback with himself or follow a fixed group. Classes from 2019 on have not had time to reach Year 8, so they are left out of the career-length findings, and rookie classes 2022–2025 are still early in their careers.
